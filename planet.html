What is the model trying to remember?

Imagine controlling a cart with a pole attached. You want to swing the pole upright and balance it. Your input is a camera image; your action moves the cart left or right.

One image tells you where the pole appears to be, but may not tell you whether it is moving left or right. The agent needs a memory of earlier images and actions.

Memory: h

The recurrent network carries useful information forward through time. For the same inputs and parameters, its update is deterministic. It is a learned memory—not a guarantee that every past detail is preserved.

Stochastic state: z

A sample from a learned distribution represents the current situation and uncertainty about it. It allows different possible futures. It is more than arbitrary noise added to the system.

Full latent state = (h, z)

Latent means these are internal learned numbers, not raw pixels. Some may encode information useful for position or motion, but they do not come with human-readable labels such as “pole speed.”

Notation: this guide uses z for the stochastic component to match Dreamer terminology. In the RSSM equations of the PlaNet paper, that component is called s. Thus our (h, z) corresponds to the paper’s (h, s). A subscript t means the current time step.

Transition: the internal simulator

In RSSM, the transition has two parts: the recurrent model updates memory from the previous memory, stochastic state, and action; the prior net predicts a distribution over the new stochastic state. Together they describe what might happen after an action.

What each “model” means

A model here is a learned mathematical mapping, usually implemented by a neural network. It produces a prediction or the parameters of a probability distribution. These components cooperate and are trained together.

The reward model learns to predict the reward supplied by the task. It does not invent the goal, and it does not choose the action. PlaNet’s planner chooses actions using its reward predictions.

A reconstruction describes the same time step as the decoded state. If you decode an imagined future state, it becomes a future-image prediction. PlaNet normally skips that decoding during planning because rewards can be predicted directly from the latent state.

ht = f(ht−1, zt−1, at−1)
Prior: p(zt | ht)   ·   Posterior: q(zt | ht, ot)
Observation: p(ot | ht, zt)   ·   Reward: p(rt | ht, zt)

Read “|” as “given.” The prior is a prediction before using the current observation; the posterior is an observation-informed estimate. Both are learned distributions, not two separate copies of the true environment state.

How RSSM learns from real experience

PlaNet stores sequences of images, actions, and rewards. It samples short sequences from that dataset and processes them in time order. In each step:

1

Remember the past. Update h using the previous h, the previous sampled z, and the recorded action.

2

Predict before looking. The prior estimates a distribution for z from h alone.

3

Use the image. The encoder extracts image features, and the posterior uses those features together with h to estimate z. Sample the state from this posterior while processing observed data.

4

Check the predictions. Decode (h, z) to reconstruct the image and predict the reward. Compare those outputs with the recorded image and reward.

5

Connect seeing with predicting. A KL divergence compares the posterior q with the prior p. This teaches the prior to predict useful states without the image and regularizes the inferred representation so it remains predictable.

Training loss ≈ image reconstruction error + reward prediction error + KL(q || p)

This is the paper’s variational learning objective in plain language. The neural components are optimized together by gradient-based learning. The posterior is a learned estimate, not a ground-truth state label.

In the cart example, suppose memory alone predicts that the pole is still moving left, but the next image provides evidence that it has changed direction. The observation-informed state helps expose that prediction error. Repeating this across many sequences improves both state estimation and dynamics prediction.

The original implementation uses Gaussian output distributions. Image and reward negative log-likelihoods correspond to squared-error losses up to constants when variance is fixed. It also uses a “free nats” threshold on KL; that is an optimization detail, not an extra model box.

Source: PlaNet §3, training objective and Eq. 4; Appendix A.

How PlaNet turns predictions into an action

RSSM supplies the simulator. PlaNet adds the data-collection, model-training, and action-planning loop. It uses CEM, the cross-entropy method, to search for promising action sequences.

Observe→Infer current state→Imagine action sequences→Compare rewards→Act and observe again
  1. Estimate the current state using the real observation and the posterior.
  2. Sample many candidate action sequences. For the cart, each is a possible series of left/right forces.
  3. Imagine each sequence. Repeatedly update memory, sample the next stochastic state from the prior, and predict reward. No future camera images are available.
  4. Score and refine. Sum the predicted rewards along each sequence, keep the better candidates, and adjust the action-sampling distribution toward them. Repeat this search a few times.
  5. Execute only the current action from the resulting plan. After the next real observation arrives, update the state and plan again. The paper takes the current action from the mean of the final CEM distribution.

Replanning helps the agent respond when reality differs from its predictions. This is called model-predictive control.

Imagining does not require drawing imaginary pictures. Each rollout can stay in the compact latent state and predict rewards directly. That makes it practical to compare many action sequences.

The outer learning loop starts with a small dataset collected using random actions. PlaNet alternates between fitting its model and collecting more experience with its improving planner. Exploration noise is added during collection.

Source: PlaNet §2, Algorithm 1; CEM and latent-space planning; Appendix B.

The whole paper, in everyday language

1 · The problem

Can an agent learn to control an unfamiliar environment using images, without already knowing its physics? Learning by real trial and error can require many interactions. A learned simulator could let the agent try possibilities internally.

2 · The proposed agent: PlaNet

Learn compact dynamics from experience and plan directly in that compact space. Train the model, collect new experience using planning, then train again. The agent has no actor or critic network.

3 · The proposed dynamics: RSSM

Use both recurrent memory and stochastic states. A purely recurrent model does not explicitly represent different possible futures; a purely stochastic state model can struggle to preserve information over time. The combination worked better in the paper’s experiments.

4 · A training idea: latent overshooting

A good one-step prediction does not guarantee a good long rollout when model capacity is limited. Predict several steps ahead and compare the predicted latent distributions with the corresponding future posteriors inferred from observations. This adds supervision without decoding every future image.

Caveat: it helped some models, but was unnecessary for the final RSSM agent and slightly reduced RSSM performance in the reported comparison.

5 · What the experiments show

PlaNet was evaluated on six simulated continuous-control tasks: cart-pole swing-up, reaching, cheetah running, finger spinning, catching a ball in a cup, and walking. The results support combining deterministic and stochastic state components and refining plans with CEM.

PlaNet after 500 episodes achieved broadly similar performance to the D4PG baseline trained for 100,000 episodes, except on the finger task. The paper describes this as about 200 times fewer episodes.

6 · What to take from the result

A learned model can provide useful predictions for planning without perfectly recreating the world. The evidence is from the paper’s simulated tasks; it does not establish the same efficiency gain for every environment. Model errors, incomplete observations, and unfamiliar situations still matter.

Sources: PlaNet paper, §§1–5; Appendix A; Appendix D and Fig. 7 for the overshooting caveat.

Where Dreamer fits

DreamerV3 directly uses an RSSM-based world model. Its actor learns to choose actions and its critic estimates future return. Both learn from imagined trajectories starting at states inferred from real experience. Imagined steps use the prior, while newly observed real steps use the posterior.

The published DreamerV3 actor loss uses REINFORCE for both discrete and continuous actions. The actor–critic system is part of the complete agent, not an extra part of the RSSM transition itself.
